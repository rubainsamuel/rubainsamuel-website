I was recently working on an ideal customer profile for a large company whose clients span regions, industries and sizes. A conventional ICP felt too blunt for the business. The challenge was to make it specific enough to guide decisions without forcing a varied customer base into an artificially narrow definition.

Partly out of curiosity, and partly to see how well an agent could handle the problem, I gave my analysis and some of the underlying data to Claude Cowork. I asked it to analyse the patterns and develop a model that captured what made the best customers valuable without defining the profile too narrowly. After several hours and a few rounds of iteration, it came back with something quite convincing. 

The model was clear, nuanced and actionable, and I could not see an obvious flaw in it. It was better than what I had before. But what stayed with me was how easily I had accepted it.

I had framed the problem, selected the initial variables and set most of the constraints. The agent had done much of the analysis and shaped the eventual model. I then looked at what came back and decided that it made sense. Nothing about that arrangement felt irresponsible. In fact, it felt like a good example of how I want to use these tools.

But it left me with a question I have found myself returning to since. If I am still making the final decision, how do I know whether I am meaningfully exercising judgment or just approving judgment that has largely been formed for me?

## Where does agency sit in the work?

It is tempting to answer that question by measuring how much of the work the agent performed. We increasingly describe AI use in terms of how much of a task was automated, how many hours were saved or how much of an output was generated by the system. I am not sure those measures tell us very much about agency.

Albert Bandura distinguished between direct personal agency and what he called [proxy agency](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.psych.52.1.1), meaning acting through other people in pursuit of outcomes we want. Delegating the action does not necessarily mean surrendering agency over the outcome. That feels familiar in organisations. A senior executive may personally perform very little of the research, modelling or analysis behind a decision while remaining responsible for its direction and consequences. A good manager does not become less agentic because someone else built the spreadsheet.

The ICP example becomes more revealing when I ask where the delegation actually occurred. If I break the work down, there were at least three kinds of activity involved. I was deciding what problem we were trying to solve and what seemed important enough to include. There was the work of analysing the accounts and developing a model. And there was the judgment involved in deciding whether that model represented the business well enough to use. I think of these as direction, execution and evaluation.

The boundaries between them are quite murky. Choosing which patterns deserve weight is part of the analysis, but it is also a judgment about the business. Deciding how narrow the central ICP should be depends partly on what the analysis reveals, while also shaping what a good answer looks like. By the time I received the final model, some of those choices had already been made inside the process that produced it.

I could still reject the answer. I could challenge an assumption, ask for another version or rebuild the model myself. What I am less sure about is whether having the opportunity to exercise judgment is the same as actually exercising it.

## Judgment is harder to notice when it weakens

The difficulty is that not every kind of capability loss announces itself in the same way. When an execution skill starts to fade, there are often signs. Try writing something without the tool and the blank page may feel harder than it used to. Build a model from scratch and you may notice that you have forgotten how some of the pieces fit together. Removing the assistance can make the dependency visible.

Judgment is harder to test in the same way. If my ability to evaluate an analysis begins to weaken, part of what I rely on to notice that change is the same judgment that may have weakened. A poor piece of writing can expose a decline in writing ability. A bad decision does not necessarily come with an internal warning that the judgment behind it has weakened.

This creates an uncomfortable possibility. My ability to recognise a weak answer could decline without an equivalent decline in my confidence that the answer looks right.

Researchers studying automation have encountered versions of this problem for decades. Raja Parasuraman and Dietrich Manzey’s [review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055) of automation-related complacency found that people can become less effective at monitoring highly reliable automated systems, particularly when their attention is divided across other tasks. That does not necessarily reflect laziness. If a system performs reliably most of the time, allocating less attention to checking it can be a reasonable response to limited attention. As the system earns our trust, close scrutiny can start to feel less necessary.

A 2025 multicentre [observational study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40816301/) of AI-assisted polyp detection offers a more tangible example. Researchers found that after regular exposure to AI assistance, endoscopists performing colonoscopies without it had a lower adenoma detection rate than clinicians at the same centres had achieved before AI was introduced. The study cannot establish that AI caused the decline, but it raises the possibility that routine assistance affected performance when that assistance was removed.

Medicine is useful here because the change could be measured against an observable outcome.

In strategy, the feedback is rarely that direct or measurable. If I become slightly worse at noticing that a market model rests on the wrong assumption, there may be no immediate signal telling me so. A recommendation can be analytically coherent and still be wrong for this organisation or this moment. It may pass through several meetings looking entirely reasonable, and the decision itself may take months or years to play out. By then, its quality is tangled up with execution, competitors, market conditions and everything else that happened afterwards.

That makes independent judgment harder to audit. I can review an agent’s analysis, question parts of it and remain accountable for the decision without knowing whether my own ability to evaluate the work is becoming less sharp.

## Some of the friction was doing real work

Delegating work to another person has always carried some friction. A colleague has limited time and other priorities. Asking them to revisit an analysis means using more of that time, and usually requires some explanation of what needs to change. There is a natural limit to how many times I can say “try again” without becoming clearer about what I am looking for.

An agent changes that equation. It has no competing priorities, no impatience and no memory of the social debt I accumulated by asking for version nine. The marginal cost of another attempt can be close to zero. I can ask for a different interpretation, another analysis or five alternative structures simply because I am not quite satisfied with what I have. While that is enormously useful, it also changes what I have to contribute between iterations.

In the ICP exercise, I could keep refining the model without having to formulate a complete alternative myself. Each round could begin with a reaction to what Claude had produced: This feels too narrow. That distinction seems artificial. Can you find a cleaner way to represent these accounts? Those reactions contain judgment, but reacting to possibilities someone else has generated is different from having to construct the possibilities yourself. Over enough iterations, I can remain deeply involved in the work while spending more of my effort selecting, correcting and steering ideas the agent has already put in front of me.

One of the advantages of these systems is that they make exploration cheap. A 2025 [field experiment](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2025.20702) involving 791 professionals at Procter & Gamble found that individuals working with generative AI could perform at levels comparable to two-person teams without AI on product-development tasks. That suggests some of the benefit we once gained by adding another person can now be accessed without the time and coordination that collaboration usually requires.

Much of that friction is worth removing. Waiting for someone else’s time, coordinating another round of work or hesitating to ask for help are not good ways to improve thinking. But those constraints had a secondary effect, they forced us to pause, explain what was wrong, decide what we wanted next and sometimes work through the alternative ourselves. As agents remove those constraints, some of the moments that previously required us to form a clearer view can disappear with them. The process can stop requiring those moments without anyone consciously deciding to give them up.

## What would it mean to design for agency?

If moments that exercise judgment can disappear without us noticing, preserving agency means being deliberate about where the process still requires us to form and apply our own judgment, rather than merely choose from options the agent has already framed for us.

That will not always mean forming an answer before consulting the agent. Sometimes I use an agent precisely because I do not yet know what I think. I want it to surface possibilities I have missed, challenge the way I framed the problem or show me evidence that should change my view. In other cases, independent judgment may enter earlier. Before asking an agent to assess several strategic options, I might decide which criteria should matter and why. In other situations, I might ask the agent to generate competing interpretations without asking it to choose between them.

What I want to avoid is a process in which the agent defines the question, develops the answer and then supplies the reasons I use to decide that the answer is good. I may still have final approval in that arrangement, but increasingly little in the process requires me to form a view of my own.

I have started to see the value of writing down context, criteria and working principles differently because of this. I originally thought of them mainly as ways to improve the agent’s output. Clearer instructions usually produce better work and reduce the amount of correction required later. But writing them down also makes me articulate some of my own judgment before I see the answer. I have to decide what matters, what trade-offs I am willing to make and where I am uncertain.

Of course, judgment cannot be fully documented in advance. Context changes, exceptions matter and evidence can expose weaknesses in the criteria themselves. If every important judgment could be reduced to a rule, there would be little judgment left to exercise.

The aim, then, is to preserve places in the work where judgment still has to happen.

## Where does judgment actually live?

The more I think about this, the less convinced I am that the task itself is the right unit for deciding what to delegate. Research, analysis, drafting and decision-making are useful labels for organising work, but they tell us surprisingly little about where judgment is actually being exercised.

Two pieces of analysis can demand very different things. One may involve applying a familiar method to structured data. Another may require deciding which evidence deserves weight or choosing between competing objectives where there is no clearly correct answer.

That suggests we need a more practical way to identify the moments in a piece of work where judgment actually has to be exercised. I would find real value in a framework that helps map where someone has to frame the problem, decide what evidence deserves weight and make trade-offs that cannot simply be resolved by applying a rule. That would give us a better basis for deciding what to delegate.

I do not yet know what such a framework should look like, and it would almost certainly vary across roles, experience levels and types of work. But as agents become more autonomous and capable of carrying work across multiple stages on our behalf, I increasingly think we will need one.

The key question will be less about which tasks remain human, and more about which acts of judgment people still need to exercise themselves if they are to remain capable of directing, challenging and even correcting the agents working for them.
